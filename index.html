Road to Revolution: Making History Playable

Jinheng Wu • Additional Materials for College Admissions
“For the game Road to Revolution, I translated American Revolution-era alliances history into character abilities, factions, and victory conditions, then ran over 10,000 Python simulations to test the game balance. Initial trials showed that the General faction won 76.68% of four-player games, highlighting an imbalance and informing proposed revisions. Making history playable forced me to ask what disappears when complex events become rules, heroes, and winners.”

Data does not settle history, but I can use it to locate patterns and absences—then return to the archive to find what the numbers cannot explain. Below is the original physical rulebook, playable card deck, quantitative simulation results, and critical historiographical reflections.

3. Quantitative Simulation & Game Balance (10,000 Trials)

Empirical findings from a custom Python Monte Carlo simulation engine executing complete turn loops, equipment math, and hidden-role targeting.

Initial 4-Player Simulation (10,000 Games)

General Team (Washington / Continental) 76.68% (7,805 wins)
Enemy Faction (Loyalist / Crown) 16.58% (1,658 wins)
Rebel / Opportunist 5.33% (533 wins)
Simulation Finding: In 64.5% of games, the death sequence followed Enemy → Rebel. The General team enjoyed overwhelming early-game tempo because enemy players bore the brunt of hidden-role targeting before the rebel could pivot. This quantitative diagnosis drove specific balance changes: adjusting defense-to-attack ratios in the deck, enhancing French covert subsidies, and tuning minority faction comeback cards.

4. Critical Reflection: Lossy Compression

Excerpt from personal design essay reflecting on turning historical reality into rules.

“When I designed a card game about the American Revolution, I thought the difficult part would be balancing the game. Instead, I became stuck on a much stranger problem: how do I turn a real person into a rule?

For an event like the Boston Massacre, a card needs to produce a clear effect. Someone gains an advantage, someone loses one, and the game continues. But the real event did not happen that neatly. The five people who died had different lives, reasons for being there, and experiences that could not fit into one line of game text. The better I made the rule, the more I noticed what the rule had removed.

I started thinking of this as putting knowledge ‘into water.’ A historical narrative, a theory, or a model can look complete when it stays on paper. Once I put it into another situation—a game, a conversation, or something I actually experience—it starts to leak. The gaps become easier to see.

This is also why I have become interested in the idea of lossy compression. We compress complicated things all the time because we have to. A game compresses history into mechanics. Statistics compress experience into numbers... Data does not settle history, but I can use it to locate patterns and absences—then return to the archive to find what the numbers cannot explain.”
